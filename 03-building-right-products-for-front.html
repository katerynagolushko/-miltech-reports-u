Field-Driven Product Development

Ukrainian defense companies work directly with military units from day one, not in labs. The standard pattern: founder visits brigade, identifies specific problem (e.g., FPV drone jammers ineffective against fiber-optic control), builds prototype in 2-4 weeks, delivers to same unit for combat testing, iterates based on battlefield data within days. This cycle repeats until the product either proves itself under fire or fails and gets abandoned.

Products that survive 3-4 combat iterations typically see rapid scale. FPV drones evolved from garage projects to commodity products in under 18 months. The innovation frontier shifted to AI targeting systems, fiber-optic control, mesh networks, and autonomous swarms. Technologies that would require years of lab testing in Western countries prove themselves (or fail) within combat cycles measured in weeks.

Military units now serve as co-developers, not just customers. Brigades test products, provide detailed feedback, and often fund development through direct contracts. The Army Plus app enables mass feedback collection from soldiers. Products with poor field performance get filtered out by military ratings before reaching formal procurement, creating a quality control mechanism that traditional tender systems lack.

R&D Methodologies Under Rapid Growth

Arthur Seletskiy, speaking on R&D methodology selection, emphasizes that traditional waterfall development fails in defense tech because requirements change based on weekly battlefield dynamics. Teams using agile frameworks with 1-2 week sprints can respond to evolving threats. The key is maintaining direct communication channels with military units so feedback reaches engineers within 24-48 hours, not months.

Oleh Kravchuk discusses adapting US and EU methodologies for Ukrainian scale. Western defense contractors optimize for regulatory compliance; Ukrainian teams optimize for battlefield effectiveness. This creates a fundamental difference in product philosophy. A US defense product might spend 18 months in certification before first field deployment. A Ukrainian product deploys to combat in week 4, fails, gets redesigned, and proves itself by month 3.

The trade-off is technical debt. Companies that move fast accumulate code that needs refactoring, documentation gaps, and prototype components in production systems. The successful pattern is: move fast to prove product-market fit (6 months), then spend 3-6 months hardening the proven design before scaling to mass production. Teams that skip the hardening phase face catastrophic failure rates when scaling from 100 units to 10,000 units.

Key Takeaways for Defense Tech Founders

1. Build with brigades from day one, not in isolation

Standard cycle: visit brigade → identify problem → prototype (2-4 weeks) → combat test → iterate based on battlefield data. Products that survive 3-4 combat iterations see rapid scale. Military units serve as co-developers through direct contracts and Army Plus feedback app.

2. 6-month innovation cycles vs. 5-7 years in Europe

FPV drones: garage projects to commodity in 18 months. Innovation frontier shifted to AI targeting, fiber-optic control, mesh networks, swarms. Western products spend 18 months in cert before field deployment; Ukrainian products deploy week 4, iterate, prove by month 3.

3. Agile with 1-2 week sprints, not waterfall

Requirements change based on weekly battlefield dynamics. Direct communication with military units so feedback reaches engineers in 24-48 hours. Traditional waterfall fails because threats evolve faster than development plans.

4. Move fast to prove fit, then harden before scaling

Prove product-market fit in 6 months, then spend 3-6 months hardening before mass production. Teams that skip hardening face catastrophic failure when scaling 100→10,000 units. Technical debt is acceptable during validation, fatal during scale.

5. Field performance ratings filter quality pre-procurement

Army Plus app enables mass soldier feedback collection. Products with poor field performance get filtered by military ratings before formal procurement. Creates quality control mechanism that tender systems lack. Bad products die in field testing, not after delivery.
