How State Projects Actually Start

Oleksa Boyko, Lead PM at the Main IT Directorate of Ukraine's Ministry of Defence (ex-National Guard aerial recon 2022-24, ex-iOS engineer, launched State Register + UAS battalion), reveals the informal reality. State projects start with "a WhatsApp group chat is created" followed by "Colleagues, we need to launch..." The real question becomes "what are we doing?" which usually means "something we don't yet know how to do but badly want," to be done "fast, high-quality, minimal resources, and secure."

Before engaging, assess the prospect: Who initiated contact? Who is the project sponsor? Is funding present? What's their reputation? If all signals are positive, prepare a pitch deck. Boyko's hints: study the problem and find hard numbers, run user surveys, conduct deep user interviews, show prior expertise and similar delivered projects. Generic proposals without specific data die in the first meeting.

Boyko's credentials matter for credibility of this framework. He stood up a UAS battalion plus 5 UAS platoons (approximately 560+150 personnel), achieved 80+ armored vehicles destroyed, and launched 10+ apps with 1M+ users. This is practitioner knowledge, not policy theory. When he describes stakeholder dynamics, he's mapping relationships from inside the Ministry.

Key Stakeholders: Authority and Engagement Cadence

Boyko outlines three critical roles on the state customer side, each with distinct motivations, responsibilities, authority levels, and engagement frequencies:

1. PROJECT SPONSOR (Deputy Ministers, Ministers, CDTO)

Motivation: public result, ministry KPIs. Authority: initiate project, pull in resources, advocate regulation changes. Engage: every 1-2 weeks. The "last-hope" escalation path when blockers can't be resolved at PM or customer level. Don't burn this channel on operational issues; save it for strategic barriers like budget approval, inter-ministry coordination, or regulatory obstacles.

2. PROJECT MANAGER (state expert, unit head, or vendor-side PM)

Motivation: hit project goals, balance stakeholder vs team interests. Engage: daily or several times per week. Your primary operational contact. Responsible for requirements translation, team coordination, timeline management, and acceptance testing. If this relationship fails, the project fails regardless of sponsor support.

3. CUSTOMER / CONSUMER (usually process owner, unit head)

Motivation: "nothing breaks and life gets easier." Owns: functional requirements, spec approval, rollout, feedback. Engage: several times per week. The end user who will actually operate the system. They care about workflow improvement, not technical elegance. Spec what makes their job easier, not what's architecturally pure.

Beyond these three core roles, four other stakeholders are CRITICAL or HIGH priority: Ministry of Digital Transformation (owns national informatization, approves budgets, administers Trembita integration), State Service for Special Comms & Info Protection / DSSZZІ (info protection approval, procurement authorization, security clearance), Donors (progress updates for funded projects), Cabinet of Ministers (regulation changes via resolutions). Ministry of Justice handles regulation approval and registries. Verkhovna Rada (parliament) is LOW frequency but required for law changes.

Entry Strategy: Before vs. During Procurement

BEFORE official procurement: Pros: faster delivery, early market lock-in. Cons: no funding at start, shaky legal field, stronger competition from other vendors positioning for the same opportunity. This path suits teams with runway to invest in speculative development, strong sponsor relationships, and ability to deliver prototypes that create switching costs before procurement opens.

DURING official procurement: Pros: fixed requirements, payment guaranteed by the state. Use framework ("рамкові") agreements for flexibility. Cons: procurement can fall through, frequent requirement changes mid-contract, key stakeholder leaving their post. Boyko emphasizes framework agreements as the flexibility mechanism. Standard tenders lock requirements rigidly; framework agreements allow scope adjustments within budget envelopes.

The hybrid approach: start BEFORE to build stakeholder trust and technical proof-of-concept, then formalize DURING through procurement once the solution is validated. Teams that win major contracts typically spent 3-6 months in pre-procurement relationship building, not just bid preparation. The decision is made informally before the tender opens; the tender executes the pre-decided outcome.

Procurement Channels and DOT-Chain

Olena Dushyna (CEO, L7 Defense) maps six parallel procurement channels in the Ukrainian defense market: (1) MoD central procurement, (2) Brave1 marketplace, (3) DOT-Chain weapons platform, (4) volunteer funds (Come Back Alive, Prytula Foundation with $100M+ budgets), (5) local administration contracts, (6) Danish Model (international government funding for Ukrainian procurement). Each channel has different speed, requirements, and payment reliability.

Alona Zhuzha describes DOT-Chain Defence as a weapons marketplace applying market approaches to public procurement. DOT-Chain enables competitive bidding with rapid evaluation cycles, transparent vendor ratings, and field performance feedback integration. Unlike traditional Prozorro tenders that optimize for lowest price, DOT-Chain incorporates quality signals from military unit ratings and battlefield performance data.

Daria Kotova outlines common mistakes when selling to the state in miltech: (1) assuming lowest price wins (true for Prozorro, false for direct contracts and DOT-Chain), (2) ignoring codification requirements until too late (16-month process can kill cash flow), (3) failing to build relationships with actual military units before approaching MoD central procurement, (4) not understanding the difference between outsource, outstaff, licensing, and grant models. Each cooperation format has distinct legal structures, payment terms, and IP ownership implications.

Project Lifecycle and What Contractors Need at Each Stage

Boyko breaks the project lifecycle into four phases with specific contractor actions required:

1. INITIATION (Decision to build): Contractor researches problem, presents solution, prepares for potential tender. Key deliverable: pitch deck with hard numbers, user interviews, and similar project examples.

2. CONCEPT (Requirements formation): Bring in Business Analyst for technical specification, cost estimation, tender documentation. This is where scope gets locked (for standard tenders) or framework boundaries set (for flexible agreements).

3. DEVELOPMENT (Build + info-protection measures): Report progress regularly, coordinate acceptance testing with DSSZZІ for security approval. Failure to involve DSSZZІ early means rejection at delivery, wasting the entire dev cycle.

4. SUPPORT & OPERATION (Scale, ensure value): Prepare for new tenders covering ongoing support, feature expansion, scaling. The initial contract likely won't cover long-term maintenance; plan the support revenue stream from day one.

Critical regulations to know: PKMU №205 (21.02.2025) on software products, PKMU №1178 (12.10.2022) on public procurement, PKMU №373 (29.03.2006) on info protection in systems. Boyko notes that MoD is hiring civilian PMs, Product Owners, BAs, DevOps, and SysAdmins with draft reservation (бронювання), indicating internal capacity building for digital transformation.

Key Takeaways for Defense Tech Founders

1. Projects start informally ("WhatsApp group"), formalize later

"Colleagues, we need to launch..." → assess: Who initiated? Who sponsors? Is funding present? Reputation check. If positive, pitch deck with hard numbers, user surveys, deep interviews, prior similar projects. Generic proposals die in first meeting. Decisions made informally pre-tender; tender executes pre-decided outcome.

2. Three core roles with distinct engagement cadence

Sponsor (Deputy Ministers, CDTO): 1-2 week engagement, last-hope escalation for strategic blockers. PM (state expert or vendor): daily/multi-weekly, operational contact. Customer (process owner): several/week, owns requirements and feedback. Burn sponsor channel = project death. Fail PM relationship = project death regardless of sponsor support.

3. Critical approvals: MinDigital (budget), DSSZZІ (security), Donors

Ministry of Digital Transformation: national informatization program, budget approval, Trembita integration. DSSZZІ: info protection approval, procurement authorization, security clearance. Involve DSSZZІ early in dev, not at delivery. Failure = rejection after full build cycle, total waste.

4. Six parallel channels: MoD, Brave1, DOT-Chain, volunteer funds, local admin, Danish Model

Different speed, requirements, payment reliability per channel. DOT-Chain: competitive bidding, rapid cycles, transparent ratings, field performance integration (not just lowest price like Prozorro). Volunteer funds: faster deployment, direct brigade relationships. Framework agreements allow scope flex within budget.

5. Common fatal mistakes: ignoring codification (16mo process), no military unit relationships, wrong cooperation format

16-month codification can kill cash flow if not planned (L7 Defense example). Approaching MoD central before building brigade relationships = rejection. Four cooperation formats (outsource, outstaff, licensing, grant) have distinct legal, payment, IP implications. Assuming lowest price wins = wrong for direct contracts and DOT-Chain.
